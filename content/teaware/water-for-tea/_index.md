## The Mother of Tea

{{< img src="water-for-tea-1.jpg" w="1200x" alt="Ink wash painting of clear spring water flowing over stones into a pool beside a porcelain gaiwan, with misty mountains behind" >}}

There is an old Chinese saying: **水为茶之母** — "water is the mother of tea." A cup is somewhere between 98% and 99% water, so the leaf you fuss over only ever gets to speak through whatever you pour on it. Brew a fine Longjing in flat, chlorinated tap water and you will taste flat, chlorinated tap water with a hint of tea.

The tea master Lu Yu understood this twelve centuries ago. In the *Cha Jing* (茶经, *The Classic of Tea*, c. 760 CE) he ranked water sources plainly: **山水上，江水中，井水下** — mountain spring water is best, river water is middle, well water is worst. Working from taste alone, a thousand years before anyone could measure a mineral, he arranged water in almost exactly the order modern chemistry would.

## What Water Actually Does

Three things in your water decide how the cup turns out.

**Chlorine and chloramine.** Municipal tap water is disinfected, and those disinfectants have an unmistakable flat, faintly chemical smell that drowns delicate aromatics. This is the single easiest fault to fix.

**Dissolved minerals (hardness).** Calcium and magnesium are the big two. In moderation, magnesium acts as a carrier that lifts sweetness and body, while a little calcium buffers sharp edges. In excess — "hard" water — calcium and magnesium bicarbonates react with tea polyphenols to form insoluble complexes. That is the source of the greasy *scum* film on the surface, the dull color, and the muddy, flat taste. Hard water's alkalinity also raises the brew's pH, flattening the bright, high notes in green and oolong teas.

**Total dissolved solids (TDS).** A rough sum of everything dissolved in the water. Too high and minerals bully the tea's subtle notes; too low — as in distilled water — and there is nothing to carry flavor out of the leaf, leaving the cup thin and one-dimensional. Tea, like tea drinkers, wants a middle path.

## A Field Guide to Water Types

| Water | Rough TDS | Verdict |
|---|---|---|
| **Spring / bottled spring** | 30–120 ppm | **Best all-rounder.** Naturally balanced minerals; look for a "soft" or low-mineral label. |
| **Filtered tap (carbon jug/faucet)** | varies | **Best value.** Removes chlorine and some hardness. Great if your tap isn't extreme. |
| **Tap water** | often 150+ ppm | Usable if soft and low-chlorine; poor if hard or heavily treated. |
| **Distilled / RO (pure)** | ~0–10 ppm | Avoid alone — tea comes out flat and hollow. |
| **Mineral water (hard)** | 300+ ppm | Avoid — scum, dull color, muted aroma. |

The sweet spot most tea people converge on: **TDS around 30–100 ppm, near-neutral pH (about 6.5–7.5), and no chlorine.** Put in mineral terms, that's calcium roughly 15–70 mg/L and magnesium roughly 4–25 mg/L.

## What to Actually Use

You don't need a laboratory. In order of effort:

1. **Cheapest fix first:** run tap water through a carbon filter jug. Removing chlorine alone is a bigger upgrade than most people expect.
2. **Reliable default:** buy a soft, low-mineral bottled spring water and keep it for tea. Check the label's mineral figures against the ranges above once, then stop worrying.
3. **If your tap is very hard or very treated**, don't chase it with more filtering — just switch to bottled spring water for tea. Reverse-osmosis or distilled water is the exception, not a goal: it's *too* empty, and only becomes good tea water if remineralized.

Two habits matter as much as the water itself:

- **Draw it fresh.** Water that has sat, or been boiled twice, loses dissolved oxygen and tastes noticeably duller — the "flat" of a reboiled kettle. Fill with cold, fresh water each session and heat it once.
- **Match temperature to the leaf.** Delicate greens and yellows want roughly 75–85°C; whites and lighter oolongs 85–90°C; roasted oolongs, reds, and pu-erh a full ~95–100°C. Good water at the wrong temperature still scalds a green tea bitter.

---

## The Lazy Truth

You can spend a small fortune on leaf and undo all of it with the water. The reverse is also true: a modest tea in clean, well-balanced water often outshines an expensive one drowned in hard tap. Fix the water first — it's the cheapest, highest-return change you can make to every cup you'll ever brew.
