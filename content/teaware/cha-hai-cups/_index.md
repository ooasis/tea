## The Half of the Table Nobody Shops For

Everyone agonizes over the first gaiwan and the first zisha pot. Almost nobody thinks about what the tea lands in after it leaves them — and that is where most beginner gongfu sessions quietly go wrong. A pitcher, a set of small cups, and a strainer cost a fraction of a pot and fix more problems than an upgrade ever will.

Here is what each one actually does, and how to pick it without overspending.

---

## The Cha Hai (公道杯): Fairness in a Glass

The **cha hai** (茶海, "sea of tea") is also called the **gong dao bei** (公道杯) — literally "cup of impartiality" — and the name is the whole explanation.

Pour straight from a gaiwan into four cups and the first cup gets thin liquor, the last gets the concentrated dregs, and meanwhile the leaves in the gaiwan keep steeping while you work your way down the row. Decant everything into the pitcher first, swirl once, then fill the cups, and every guest drinks the same tea. Even brewing alone this matters: emptying the vessel completely is what stops a gongfu infusion from over-steeping into bitterness between rounds.

**How to choose one:**

- **Volume before anything else.** Your pitcher must hold *more* than your brewing vessel — a 150 ml gaiwan wants a 200–250 ml pitcher, or you will be stopping mid-pour.
- **Glass for your first one.** It works with every tea, adds nothing to the flavor, and lets you read the liquor color, which is half of learning to judge a steep.
- **Pour, then handle.** Check that the spout cuts cleanly rather than dribbling down the outside. A handle is comfort; a clean-breaking lip is function. Thick-walled ceramic holds heat better and is worth it for aged dark teas that cool fast.

## Cups: Small on Purpose

Chinese tasting cups typically run **25–100 ml**, and the small size is not decoration. Gongfu uses a high leaf-to-water ratio and many short infusions; a small cup means you finish a round while it is still hot and move to the next, tasting how the tea evolves rather than drinking one cooling mug of it.

Material changes what you taste:

| Material | What it does | Best for |
|---|---|---|
| **Thin porcelain** | Non-absorbent, sheds heat fast, shows liquor color truthfully | The default — fragrant greens, whites, oolongs |
| **Glass** | Neutral, shows color, no aroma lift | Watching the liquor; casual use |
| **Porous clay (zisha and kin)** | Absorbs and returns aroma over time; softens edges | Roasted oolong, shu pu-erh, aged dark tea — one tea type per cup |
| **Thick glazed stoneware** | Holds heat, mutes high notes | Dark teas, cold rooms |

The rule mirrors teapots: **impervious materials for aromatic teas, porous materials for roasted and fermented ones.** A porous cup will carry last month's pu-erh into today's Longjing, so dedicate it or stay with porcelain.

If you buy one set, make it thin white porcelain around 40 ml. A white interior is the only way to see a dull, muddy liquor for what it is.

### Aroma cups, and whether you need one

**Wen xiang bei** (闻香杯) — aroma cups — come from Taiwan and Fujian and always arrive in pairs: a tall narrow cup and a short wide one. Tea goes into the tall cup, the wide cup caps it, you invert the pair, lift the tall cup away, and smell the empty vessel while you drink.

They are genuinely revealing on Taiwanese and Fujian oolongs, whose high aromatics linger in the warm cup. On pu-erh, red tea, or green tea there is much less to find. Skip them until an oolong makes you curious.

## Strainers (茶漏): The Cheapest Fix on the Table

Most gaiwans and teapots filter only the large leaf at the spout. What gets through is the fine dust — and fine dust suspended in your cup reads as cloudiness, grit on the tongue, and a harsh finish people usually blame on the tea.

A **cha lou** sits on the pitcher's mouth and catches it.

- **Stainless mesh** — the workhorse. Double-layer coarse-plus-fine mesh handles everything, rinses clean, costs little.
- **Porcelain with pierced holes** — holes drilled in the ceramic instead of a mesh. Prettier, easier to clean, coarser filtration; fine for whole-leaf oolong, not for broken red tea.
- **Cloth or bamboo** — traditional and beautiful; cloth stains and must be dried thoroughly or it sours.

Buy the stainless mesh. It matters most exactly where dust is worst: broken-grade red teas, shu pu-erh, and anything chipped off a compressed cake.

## A Sensible First Set

| Piece | Pick | Why |
|---|---|---|
| Cha hai | Glass, 200–250 ml, clean spout | Universal, shows color, outsizes your gaiwan |
| Cups | 4 × thin white porcelain, ~40 ml | Honest color, no flavor carry-over |
| Strainer | Stainless double mesh | Removes the grit you'd otherwise blame on the leaf |
| Later | Aroma cups, a clay cup | Only once a specific tea asks for it |

Add a towel and a tray you don't mind wetting and the table is complete. Everything beyond this is preference, not performance — the pitcher and the strainer are the two pieces that measurably change what reaches your mouth.
