## The Easiest Brewing Method You're Not Using

Cold brewing (冷泡茶) is almost embarrassingly simple: put leaves in cold water, put the bottle in the fridge, wait, drink. No kettle, no timer, no temperature judgment. And unlike iced tea — which is hot tea poured over ice, and usually tastes like it — cold brewing is a genuinely different extraction that produces a different drink.

The reason it works isn't convenience. It's chemistry.

## Why Cold Water Tastes Sweeter

Tea's compounds don't all dissolve at the same rate. Cold water is a selective solvent, and it happens to select for the pleasant ones.

**Caffeine and EGCG** — the catechin most responsible for astringent grip — need heat to come out efficiently. In cold water around 10°C, EGCG extraction drops to roughly a fifth of what a hot steep pulls, and a cup of cold-brewed green runs in the neighborhood of 10–20 mg of caffeine versus 25–35 mg hot-brewed from the same leaf.

**Theanine and the amino acids** — the source of umami, body, and sweetness — dissolve readily in cold water. So does EGC, a gentler catechin: in one comparison, a four-hour cold brew released about 148% of the EGC that a five-minute hot steep did.

Strip out the bitter compounds, keep the sweet ones, and you get what cold brewers always report: a soft, sweet, almost juicy infusion with no edge to it. This also makes cold brewing the most forgiving method there is. You cannot scald the leaves, you cannot over-steep into bitterness, and a tea that turns harsh in your gaiwan may be perfectly pleasant cold.

The trade-off is honest: it's a different profile, not a superior one. Roast, char, and the deep caramelized notes of a fired oolong or a well-aged shou pu-erh largely stay in the leaf. Cold brewing rewards aromatic, high-amino-acid teas and under-serves teas whose character comes from fire.

## The Method

**Ratio:** 1g of leaf per 100–150ml of water. Use 1:100 for a fuller cup, 1:150 for something lighter and more thirst-quenching. That's about 5g in a standard 500ml bottle.

**Water:** cold, straight from the fridge or filter. Same rule as hot brewing — if your tap water makes bad tea hot, it makes bad tea cold. See our [water guide]({{< ref "teaware/water-for-tea" >}}).

**Vessel:** any sealable bottle or jar. A wide mouth makes it easier to get spent leaves out afterward.

**Then:** add leaf, fill with water, seal, shake once, refrigerate. Strain or decant when done — don't leave leaves sitting in the finished tea for days.

### Timing by tea type

| Tea type | Fridge time | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| Green, yellow | 4–6 hours | The category cold brewing flatters most |
| White | 6–8 hours | Silver Needle cold-brewed is a revelation |
| Light/green oolong | 6–8 hours | Floral Tieguanyin and high-mountain types shine |
| Red (black) | 6–8 hours | Aromatic types (Jin Jun Mei, Dianhong) over malty ones |
| Roasted oolong, pu-erh, dark | 8–12 hours | Workable, but you lose most of the roast and depth |

Overnight is the practical default for almost everything: load a bottle before bed, drink it the next day.

## Which Teas to Use

**Best candidates:** delicate greens like [Longjing]({{< ref "tea-varieties/green/longjing" >}}) and [Biluochun]({{< ref "tea-varieties/green/biluochun" >}}), [Bai Hao Yinzhen]({{< ref "tea-varieties/white/baihao-yinzhen" >}}) and other whites, [Jasmine tea]({{< ref "tea-varieties/green/jasmine" >}}), and lightly oxidized oolongs.

**A practical use for mediocre leaf:** cold brewing is unusually kind to green tea that's slightly past its prime, or to a cheap tea that turns bitter when hot. It won't create flavor that isn't there, but it will stop the tea punishing you.

**Rebrewing:** good whole-leaf tea will take a second cold fill, though it comes out noticeably thinner. One reuse is usually the limit.

## Food Safety: The One Rule That Matters

Hot brewing at ~95°C sterilizes as it steeps. Cold brewing does not — and brewed tea is a perfectly good medium for bacteria, which multiply fastest between 4°C and 60°C.

So: **cold brew in the refrigerator, not on the counter.** This is the single non-negotiable. Don't hold brewed tea at room temperature for more than a few hours, keep the finished tea sealed and chilled, and drink it within about 24–48 hours (72 at the outside). If you want it faster than the fridge allows, make hot tea and chill it instead.

## Worth Trying This Week

Weigh 5g of whatever green or white tea has been sitting in your cupboard, drop it in a 500ml bottle of cold water, and leave it in the fridge overnight. It costs you thirty seconds and tells you something real about the leaf — how sweet it is underneath, how much of what you taste hot is actually just heat.
