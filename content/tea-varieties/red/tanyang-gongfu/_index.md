## The Tea That Taught Europe to Say "Congou"

If you have read old British tea catalogues, you have met this tea under a name you probably did not recognize: **Panyang Congou**. "Congou" is the export-era spelling of **工夫 (gōngfu)** — skill, patient labor — and it describes how the tea is made, not how it is brewed. A gongfu red tea is one sorted and rolled leaf by leaf until every strand in the tin looks like its neighbor. It has nothing to do with gongfu *brewing*, the small-pot method; the shared word is a coincidence that trips up nearly everyone.

Tanyang Gongfu was first produced around **1851** in Tanyang Village, Fu'an, in northeastern Fujian, when local makers adapted their leaf from green-tea processing to the red-tea methods then sweeping the province. It went out through the river port at Sanduao to Western Europe and Russia, and in **1915 it took a medal at the Panama-Pacific International Exposition in San Francisco** — the same expo that made several Chinese teas famous abroad.

## Where It Sits: The Three Minhong Gongfu Reds

Fujian produces three classic "gongfu" red teas, known collectively as **Minhong (闽红)**. They are close cousins and constantly confused in shop listings:

| Tea | Region | Character |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Tanyang Gongfu (坦洋工夫)** | Fu'an | The benchmark. Brisk and sweet, with a clean high aroma and a golden ring on the liquor. |
| **Zhenghe Gongfu (政和工夫)** | Zhenghe | Roundest and heaviest body; made from the same big-leaf cultivar as Zhenghe white tea, so expect more golden tips. |
| **Bailin Gongfu (白琳工夫)** | Fuding | Thicker, darker liquor with a more floral top note and a shorter finish. |

If a vendor simply says "Fujian black tea," ask which of the three it is. They are not interchangeable.

## The Cultivar Question

The traditional plant is **Tanyang Caicha (坦洋菜茶)**, a local small-leaf population variety — not a single clone, but a village seed-grown mixture, which is part of why older Tanyang material tastes layered rather than uniform. Since the 1980s producers have also used newer cultivars, most visibly **Jin Mudan (金牡丹, "Golden Peony")**, an aromatic cultivar that pushes the tea toward bright florals and honey.

This matters when you shop. Caicha-based Tanyang leans toward dried longan, brown sugar, and a soft malt; Jin Mudan versions are noticeably perfumed, almost oolong-like on the nose. Neither is "correct" — but if you order a second bag expecting the first one's taste and the cultivar changed, you will be surprised.

## How to Judge a Good One

| Feature | Good | Avoid |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Dry leaf** | Thin, tightly twisted, even in length, black with an oily sheen; some golden tips | Broken fragments, dull grey leaf, heavy dust at the bottom of the bag |
| **Aroma** | Sweet potato, longan, honey; florals if Jin Mudan | Sour, smoky, or flat cardboard |
| **Liquor** | Bright amber-red, clear, with a thin golden ring at the cup's rim | Cloudy, muddy, or dull brown |
| **Taste** | Sweet and brisk with low astringency; a clean, lingering finish | Sharp tannic bite, thin watery body |
| **Wet leaf** | Coppery and supple, mostly intact bud-and-leaf sets | Ragged, blackened, or uniformly shredded |

The spent leaf is the hardest thing for a seller to fake. A real gongfu red opens into recognizable bud-and-one-or-two-leaf sets, because that is what was plucked.

## Brewing: Go Cooler Than You Think

Tanyang Gongfu is the rare Chinese red tea that is better *below* boiling. Full 100°C water pulls out a dry edge and flattens the aroma that is the whole point of the tea.

**Western / mug method**
- 3 g per 350 ml (12 oz), **85°C / 185°F**, 2–3 minutes
- Re-steep once, adding a minute

**Gongfu / gaiwan method**
- 5 g in a 100–120 ml gaiwan, **85–90°C / 185–195°F**
- Quick rinse, then roughly 5s, 10s, 10s, 15s, 20s, 30s
- Expect six or more good infusions; the aroma sharpens around steep three

Drink it plain. The sweetness here is the selling point, and milk erases it — this is not an Assam substitute, whatever the old "congou" labels implied.

## Buying Notes

Tanyang Gongfu is widely available but inconsistently labeled. A few things worth doing:

- **Look for Fu'an (福安) on the origin line.** "Fujian black tea" alone is not enough.
- **Check for a stated cultivar.** Vendors who name Caicha or Jin Mudan generally know what they are selling.
- **Check the harvest year.** Red tea keeps far better than green, but a two-year-old Tanyang has lost much of its high aroma.
- **Buy a small size first.** Because the cultivar mix varies by producer, a 25 g sample tells you more than any description.

Specialty vendors that regularly carry Minhong reds include Teavivre, Yunnan Sourcing, and Jing Tea Shop; grades and prices shift season to season, so compare the current listings rather than assuming last year's offering is back.

---

Tanyang Gongfu is an easy tea to recommend and a hard one to buy badly once you know what to read on the label. It is sweet enough for a beginner, detailed enough to reward a gaiwan, and it carries 175 years of export history in a name most Western drinkers have been mispronouncing since the nineteenth century.
