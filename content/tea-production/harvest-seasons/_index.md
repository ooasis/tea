## The Word Before the Tea Name

Spend a few minutes on any Chinese tea vendor's site and you start noticing a word stuck to the front of the listing: *2026 Spring* Longjing. *Autumn* Tieguanyin. *Gu Hua* Sheng Pu-erh.

Most shoppers skim past it toward the tea name. That's backwards. Season is one of the few variables on the page that reliably changes what lands in your cup — more reliably than the grade letter and far more than the photography. It also changes the price, often by a factor of two or three for leaf off the same bushes.

Our [harvest guide](/tea-production/harvest/) covers the famous spring windows — Ming-qian, Yu-qian — in detail. This piece is about the rest of the calendar: what summer and autumn actually do to a leaf, and when the cheaper season is the one you want.

---

## Why Season Changes the Leaf at All

A tea bush is a plant on a budget. Through winter it sits dormant, quietly banking amino acids and sugars in its roots. When spring growth begins, it spends that savings on the first flush of buds.

That is the whole mechanism, and everything else follows from it:

- **Spring** growth is slow and funded by stored reserves. Analyses across tea types consistently find spring leaf higher in amino acids and soluble sugars than later harvests — the compounds behind sweetness, umami, and that thick, filling texture tasters call *body*.
- **Summer** growth is fast. Heat and rain push the bush to produce leaf quickly, and quickly-grown leaf is dilute and comparatively coarse. Summer tea is the most consistently criticized season across sources.
- **Autumn** growth happens under cool nights, shorter days, and — crucially — much less rain. A drier leaf releases aromatics more readily, which is why autumn tea's signature strength is fragrance rather than substance.

Chinese tea people compress all of that into four characters: **春水秋香 (chūn shuǐ qiū xiāng)** — "spring water, autumn fragrance." Spring gives you the liquor; autumn gives you the nose.

## The Three Seasons Compared

| | Spring (春茶) | Summer (夏茶) | Autumn (秋茶) |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Rough window** | Mid-March – May | June – August | September – October |
| **Leaf** | Plump buds, fine downy tips | Large, loose, coarse | Mature, sturdy, varied in color |
| **Aroma** | Fresh, floral, delicate | Flat, low | Loud, sweet, warm — hay, ripe fruit, honey |
| **Texture** | Thick, full, mouth-filling | Thin | Smooth, lighter than spring |
| **Taste** | Sweet, mellow, layered | Noticeably bitter and astringent | Clean and sweet; less depth than spring |
| **Infusions** | Many; holds up well | Few | Moderate |
| **Price** | Highest | Lowest | Mid — usually the value buy |

A caution on the chemistry: published studies disagree on which season carries more caffeine and polyphenols, with results swinging by cultivar and region. Treat any vendor's confident "autumn tea is lower in caffeine" claim as marketing, not fact. The amino acid and sweetness advantage for spring is the part the evidence actually agrees on.

## Where Autumn Wins

"Spring is better" is the default assumption, and for green tea it is simply true — buy spring, drink it young, move on. But three categories genuinely flip:

**Tieguanyin and Anxi oolong.** The autumn picking runs late October into early November, and *qiū xiāng* (autumn fragrance) is the reason Anxi producers bother with it. Spring Tieguanyin has the better liquor — rounder, sweeter, more texture. Autumn has the more arresting smell the moment you lift the lid. Many longtime drinkers buy both and reach for autumn when they want the aroma to carry a room.

**White tea, especially Shou Mei.** Autumn white is picked after *Bai Lu* (White Dew, early September). The leaf is mature, lower in moisture, and sweet in a dried-fruit-and-hay direction rather than spring's fresh-grass direction. It is also the material that ages best — if you're laying white tea down for a few years, autumn Shou Mei is the traditional and cheaper choice.

**Pu-erh, with an asterisk.** Autumn pu-erh — **谷花茶 (gǔ huā chá)**, "grain flower tea," named for the rice flowering as the rains end — is picked from September. It is softer, fruitier, friendlier to drink right now, and markedly cheaper than spring material from the same mountain. The asterisk: spring maocha is thicker and widely held to age better. Drink autumn pu-erh; press spring pu-erh into the cupboard.

## Where Autumn Loses

Green tea is the clear case against. Longjing, Biluochun, Maofeng and company are built entirely on tender early-spring buds, and there is no autumn version worth your money. If a green tea listing doesn't name its spring harvest date, assume it isn't one.

Summer tea has no advocates as a drinking tea. It isn't wasted, though — it goes into blends, into scenting bases for jasmine tea, into inexpensive ripe pu-erh, and into the commodity grades behind most tea bags. Perfectly reasonable destinations. Just not worth paying single-origin prices for.

---

## Buying Rules That Follow From This

1. **Green tea: spring, current year, always.** No exceptions worth making.
2. **Oolong: match the season to the mood.** Spring for texture and sweetness, autumn for aroma. Both are legitimate; a shop selling only one isn't telling you the whole story.
3. **Aging anything: buy spring.** Spring leaf has more to transform. This applies to pu-erh and, to a lesser degree, white tea.
4. **Drinking it this year: autumn is the value play.** Same garden, same maker, often half the price, aroma intact.
5. **No season stated? Ask.** A vendor who knows their producer will answer immediately. Silence or vagueness is itself an answer — unlabeled leaf is usually summer, or a blend across seasons.
6. **Don't pay a spring premium for autumn leaf.** On oolong and pu-erh especially, the price gap is real. Knowing the season is how you check that the price makes sense.

## A Cheap Experiment

The fastest way to make any of this stick: order a small amount of spring Tieguanyin and autumn Tieguanyin from the same vendor, ideally the same producer. Brew them side by side, same water, same leaf weight, same timing.

Smell both wet lids first — the autumn one will announce itself. Then taste. The spring cup will feel like it has more *in* it, a weight across the tongue the autumn cup doesn't match.

That single comparison teaches more than any amount of reading, and it's maybe twenty dollars. After it, "spring water, autumn fragrance" stops being a saying you've read and becomes a thing you've tasted.
